### Q: You define timescapes using a “five-window” framework. Walk us through some of those windows and why you settled on this framework for describing how animals perceive time.

**A:** The five windows are five temporal features of our experience which shape our timescapes. For example, when squirrels and starlings are made to hear recordings of calls from other members of their species that have bursts of white noise inserted into them, [the animals fill in the sound over that white noise interval](https://www.psycnet.org/record/2020-79083-006) and react to the call as if the white noise weren’t there at all. But that happens only when the white noise interval doesn’t exceed a specific duration. This duration is much shorter than it is in humans. This is what we call the window of revision, which is how long perceptual contents remain editable in light of new perceptual inputs.

### Q: What are some common misconceptions of how animals perceive time?

**A:** I think one of the biggest misunderstandings is assuming that we can understand differences using what are known as CCFTs [critical flicker fusion thresholds].==CFFT is retinal sensitivity to flickering light; it measures the flicker rate threshold beyond which you see flickering light as steady. The LED bulbs in our homes flicker at 100 or 120 hertz. We perceive them as steady because they’re flickering at a CFFT higher than our eyes can perceive. But if the same light bulb was shown to a flycatcher bird, it would most likely be able to see that it’s flickering because it has a CFFT of 140 hertz.

Some people take this fact to mean that birds may be seeing the world in slow motion compared to humans, but we think that this is a leap because we’re taking only retinal sensitivities to interpret the general speed of the experience.
